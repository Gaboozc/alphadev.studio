# Unit 4 — Proposals and Professional Email

**Weeks 8–9 · 12 hours**

**Guiding question:** How do I write a proposal and emails that the client reads, understands and answers?

**Estimated time:** Guide approx. 25 min (about 1,900 words at 120 wpm + 5 min of quick checks) · Exercises approx. 2 h 40 min (4 level-1 × 10 min, 3 level-2 × 25 min, 1 level-3 × 45 min) · Quiz approx. 30 min · Reflection approx. 1 h 30 min. **Active time approx. 5 h 05 min** of the 12 h planned in the syllabus. The remaining approx. 6 h 55 min is independent study: rereading, notes, reference readings and review.

> **Core idea.** The proposal is the last piece of sales writing before a client decides, and email is the channel where almost every professional relationship is won or lost. Both combine what you've learned: clarity (U1), honest persuasion (U2) and attention to the reader's experience (U3). In this unit you'll learn a seven-part proposal structure, how to present price ethically, and how to write emails that get read and answered.

## Objectives

- Put together a complete proposal with a seven-part structure.
- Define the scope (what's included and what isn't) to protect both sides.
- Present the price with value context and options, without deception.
- Write professional emails with effective subject lines, openings and closings.
- Write a sales email and a follow-up.

## 1. The proposal

### The most common mistake
Many proposals open like this: *"Dear [name], We are [company], founded in [year], and we specialize in…"*. The client already knows who you are (that's why they asked for the proposal). What they want to know is: **did you understand me? what exactly will I get? when? how much does it cost? why you?** Start with them, not you.

### A seven-part structure

**0. Executive summary (one page or less).** For whoever won't read the whole thing, which is usually the person who signs: problem, solution, timeline, investment and next step.

**1. The problem, in their words.** Show that you listened. Use what they said in the meeting, in their vocabulary.
> "On our call, you mentioned that 70% of your orders come in through Instagram messages and that orders get lost at peak times because nobody can reply fast enough."
(If you quote a number, make it the one they gave you.)

**2. The proposed solution.** What you'll do, in what order and why that way. Specific, with benefits (U2) and no unnecessary jargon (U1).

**3. What's included and what isn't.** The most important part for avoiding conflict. "Endless rounds of revisions" and money-losing projects usually come from a vague scope (you saw this in the North Studio case in *Critical Thinking*).
> *Included:* a catalog of up to 40 products, card and bank-transfer payments, 2 rounds of design revisions, a 1-hour training session.
> *Not included:* copywriting, product photography, integration with your accounting system (quoted separately).

**4. Timeline.** Milestones with specific dates, not "approximately 4–6 weeks." Plus the dependencies: what you need from the client and when ("copy and photos need to be ready by March 10 to keep the date").

**5. Investment.** A clear price, payment terms, options if relevant (section 2).

**6. Why us.** Not your whole portfolio: the case **most similar** to theirs, with a verifiable result and the client's permission.

**7. Next steps.** One concrete, easy action: "If this works for you, reply to this email by Friday the 15th and we'll send the contract within 24 hours so we can start Monday the 18th."

### The writing inside the proposal
- The client's language, not yours.
- Benefits first; features as proof.
- Short paragraphs, informative headings, bold only for what's key: proposals get scanned too.
- No made-up data: everything you claim (timelines, case results, market figures) has to be defensible.

## 2. Presenting price ethically

### Anchoring and value context
In *Critical Thinking* (U4) you saw **anchoring**: the first number influences how the next ones are judged. In a proposal, presenting the price next to a value reference helps the client evaluate it:

> "The investment is **$4,500**. For reference: on our call, you estimated you lose about 20 orders a month at peak times, with an average order of $30. If the system recovers half of them, that's about $300 a month in sales, every month."

Notice that the reference uses **the client's own data** and an explicit assumption ("if it recovers half"). What isn't ethical:
- Comparing with made-up market prices ("a freelancer would charge you triple") with no source.
- Promising results you can't guarantee.
- Hiding recurring costs (hosting, licenses, maintenance).

### Options
Offering two or three packages (e.g., basic, recommended, complete) lets the client choose *how* to work with you instead of *whether* to, and makes clear what each tier gets them. The options must be real: a "decoy" package designed only to make the other one look cheap borders on manipulation.

| | Basic | Recommended | Complete |
|---|---|---|---|
| Store with up to 40 products | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Messaging integration (Instagram/WhatsApp) | — | ✓ | ✓ |
| Accounting integration | — | — | ✓ |
| Investment | $3,000 | $4,500 | $6,200 |

### Calculation errors
Check every number twice (or in a spreadsheet). An error in a proposal's total destroys credibility, and if the client signs it, you may be held to it.

## 3. Professional email

### Subject line
It's the only thing that's guaranteed to be read. Say what it's about and, if there's something to do, what and by when.
> ✗ "Follow-up" · "Hi" · "Important"
> ✓ "Online store proposal: can we review it Thursday?" · "We need the copy by 3/10 to keep the launch date"

### Structure
1. A short **greeting** with their name.
2. **First line = the essentials:** the request, the news or the answer.
3. The minimum necessary **context**.
4. **Next step:** who, what, when.
5. A friendly **close** and a signature with contact details.

### Practical rules
- **One email, one topic.** If there are three unrelated topics, maybe that's three emails (or a numbered list).
- **Make it easy to reply:** questions that can be answered with yes/no or by picking an option. "Does Thursday at 11 or Friday at 10 work for you?" beats "When are you free?"
- **Tone:** friendly and direct. Neither stiffly formal ("Please be advised…") nor too casual with someone you don't know.
- **Bad news:** say it early and clearly, with the short reason, what you're doing about it and the decision you need. Don't hide it in the third paragraph.
- **Before sending:** check recipients, attachments, names, numbers and dates.

### Worked example 1 — An email with bad news
> **Subject:** Store launch: we propose moving the date to March 20
>
> Hi Mariana,
>
> We need to move the launch from March 13 to March 20. The payment provider still hasn't given us access to their system, and without it we can't turn on payments.
>
> You have two options:
> 1. **Launch on March 20** with everything working (recommended).
> 2. **Launch on March 13** with the catalog live and orders taken by message, and turn on online payments once we have access.
>
> Which do you prefer? If you let me know by Wednesday, I'll adjust the plan that same day.
>
> In the meantime, we're following up with the provider every day, and I'll let you know as soon as there's news.
>
> Best,
> Diego

## 4. The sales email

For people who already know your brand but haven't bought. Remember U2: you're not writing to convince people who don't have the problem, but to resolve the doubt of someone who's half convinced.

### Components
- **Subject line + preview text** (preheader): together they make the "first impression" in the inbox. Write several variations to test (A/B).
- **An opening that connects:** with the reader's situation, not your company.
- **Body:** a structure from U2 (PAS often works well) and one strong piece of proof (a real testimonial or case).
- **A single call to action**, clear, with what happens next.
- **Designed for mobile:** short paragraphs, a big button, text readable without zooming; check how it looks on a screen about 375 px wide.

### Worked example 2

> **Subject:** Still losing orders in your DMs?
> **Preview:** A way to answer everyone, even at the lunch rush.
>
> Hi Carlos,
>
> A month ago you downloaded our guide to selling through messaging apps. If your business is like most of the ones that write to us, the problem isn't getting messages; it's answering them all in time.
>
> Every message that goes unanswered during the lunch rush is a customer who orders somewhere else.
>
> We connect your Instagram and WhatsApp inboxes to a menu and to automatic replies so nobody's left waiting. Los Arcos Taqueria set it up in February, and the owner told us she now handles lunch orders without hiring anyone else. *(Fictional business and testimonial, created for this example. In a real email, only real testimonials published with permission.)*
>
> **[Book a 20-minute demo]**
> We'll show you how it would look with your menu. No commitment.
>
> Diego · North Studio

### The follow-up
Most emails don't get answered the first time, often just because people are busy. A short follow-up a few days later that adds something (a specific question, a useful piece of data, an easier option) is professional. Nagging without adding anything, or with guilt ("I haven't heard back…"), isn't. And if there's no reply after two or three tries, close politely and leave the door open.

> **Subject:** Re: Online store proposal
> Hi Mariana, would a 15-minute call help answer any questions about the proposal? I'm free Thursday at 11 or Friday at 10.

## 5. Using AI for proposals and emails

AI helps you structure, generate subject line variations or check the tone. But a proposal is a document with contractual consequences: **verify every number, date and commitment**, and don't let AI make up cases, testimonials or market data (*Critical Thinking*, U4; *Working with AI*, U4). Always personalize: clients recognize a generic proposal.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Opening by talking about your company | Open with the client's problem |
| Vague scope | An explicit "Included / Not included" |
| Approximate dates | Milestones with dates and dependencies |
| A price with no context, or with made-up comparisons | Value context using the client's data |
| Empty subject lines ("Follow-up") | A subject line that states the topic and the action |
| Hidden bad news | First line, with options |
| Several calls to action | One, clear |

## Summary

The proposal starts with the client's problem and follows seven parts: problem, solution, scope (what's included and what isn't), timeline, investment, why us and next steps, with an executive summary up front. Price is presented with value context based on real data and genuine options. Emails put the essentials in the subject line and first line, make replying easy and close with a clear next step; the sales email speaks to people who are already half convinced, with real proof and a single call to action.
