# Unit 2 — Writing to Persuade

**Weeks 3–5 · 18 hours**

**Guiding question:** How do I get someone to act without misleading them?

**Estimated time:** Guide approx. 25 min (about 2,100 words at 120 wpm + 5 min of quick checks) · Exercises approx. 2 h 30 min (3 level-1 × 10 min, 3 level-2 × 25 min, 1 level-3 × 45 min) · Quiz approx. 30 min · Reflection approx. 1 h 30 min. **Active time approx. 4 h 55 min** of the 18 h planned in the syllabus. The remaining approx. 13 h 05 min is independent study: rereading, notes, reference readings and review.

> **Core idea.** *Copywriting* is writing with a specific goal: getting the reader to do something (buy, subscribe, book a call, reply). It relies on three tools, which we'll cover in order: talking about **outcomes for the reader** instead of product features, using the **principles of persuasion** documented by social psychology ethically, and organizing the message with proven **structures**. All of it rests on the clarity from U1: persuasive copy that isn't understood doesn't persuade.

## Objectives

- Tell informative writing apart from copywriting.
- Turn features into benefits with the "so what?" technique.
- Write a specific, verifiable value proposition.
- Explain Cialdini's seven principles and tell ethical use from manipulation.
- Apply AIDA, PAS, FAB and BAB and choose the structure for the context.

## 1. What copywriting is (and isn't)

| Informative writing | Copywriting |
|---|---|
| Communicates information | Aims for a specific action |
| Focuses on the topic | Focuses on the reader and their problem |
| Measured by whether it was understood | Measured by whether people acted (clicks, purchases, replies) |

Copy has three jobs, in order:
1. **Catch the attention** of the right person (not everyone).
2. **Hold their interest**: whoever read the headline should want to read the next line.
3. **Prompt the action**: the reader should know exactly what to do and have reasons to do it.

### Does copy matter more than design?
You'll often read that "an ugly page with good copy outsells a pretty page with bad copy." It sounds good, but there's no general study that proves it: the outcome depends on the product, the audience and just how "ugly" the page is (a design that doesn't inspire trust drives people away too). What you can defend is this: **design and copy work together**: design guides attention and builds visual trust; copy explains the value and asks for the action. If you have to choose where to invest first on a page that doesn't convert, checking whether the message is clear and relevant is usually cheap and high-impact, and you can test it with an A/B test (*Critical Thinking*, U2).

## 2. The reader thinks about themselves: features vs. benefits

The reader isn't, at first, interested in your company, your process or your tools. They're interested in: **what's in it for me?**

- **Feature:** what the product *is* or *has*.
- **Benefit:** what the customer *gets* or *avoids* because of it.

### The "so what?" technique
Take a feature and ask "so what does that mean for the customer?" two or three times, until you reach a concrete outcome or a feeling.

### Worked example 1

*Feature:* "We build with a mobile-first responsive design."
- So what? → The site looks and works well on a phone.
- So what? → Customers can buy from their phone without getting frustrated.
- So what? → You don't lose sales from most of your visitors, who arrive on mobile.

*Benefit:* "Your customers can buy from their phone without fighting the screen."

| Feature | Benefit |
|---|---|
| "Modern tech stack (Next.js + Supabase)" | "Your site loads fast, so visitors don't leave before they see it" |
| "Strictly typed code" | "Fewer bugs in production, and fewer Friday-night surprises" |
| "24/7 support" | "If something breaks at 3 a.m., someone fixes it before your customers notice" |

**Careful:** the benefit has to be **true and provable**. "Your site loads in 0.8 seconds" can only be written if you measure it; if not, say "loads fast" or, better, measure.

**And don't drop the features:** for a technical audience, or one comparing options, they add credibility. The usual formula is *benefit first, feature as proof*: "Fewer bugs in production, thanks to strictly typed code."

## 3. The value proposition

It's the sentence (or small block) that answers: **why should someone choose you over any other option, including doing nothing?** A good value proposition:

- Says **who** it's for.
- Names the **outcome** they get.
- States **what sets you apart**.
- Is **specific**: if a competitor could copy it without changing a word, it isn't a value proposition.

> ✗ "Innovative, high-quality digital solutions for your business."
> ✓ "Online stores for independent fashion brands, ready to sell in 4 weeks, with payments and shipping already set up."

### Starter formula
"We help **[who]** **[achieve outcome]** without **[usual obstacle]**, thanks to **[differentiator]**."

It isn't meant to be copied word for word, just to make sure you have all the pieces.

## 4. Principles of persuasion (Cialdini)

Psychologist Robert Cialdini described six principles that influence decisions in *Influence* (1984); in *Pre-Suasion* (2016) he added a seventh, **unity**, which he included in the expanded edition of *Influence* (2021).

| Principle | Idea | Ethical use in copy | Abuse to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reciprocity** | We tend to return what we receive | Give real value first: a useful guide, a free assessment | "Freebies" that are traps to capture data |
| **Commitment and consistency** | We act consistently with what we've already said or done | Small steps: a quiz, a trial, before the purchase | Stringing "yeses" together to force a decision |
| **Social proof** | We look at what people like us do | Real testimonials with names, case studies, verifiable numbers | Made-up testimonials, unsupported numbers |
| **Authority** | We trust people who know | Real credentials, clients who give permission, demonstrable experience | Fake badges, invented "experts" |
| **Liking** | We prefer people we like or who are like us | Show the team, a human voice, real values | Faking closeness to manipulate |
| **Scarcity** | We value what's limited more | Real limits: "we take on 3 projects a month" (if true) | Fake countdown timers, permanent "only 2 left" |
| **Unity** | We're influenced by people we see as "one of us" | Speak from a real shared identity ("we're a small business too") | Claiming identities you don't share |

### About numbers in social proof
A typical example is: *"127 founders have already launched with us. 94% came back for a second project."* That sentence is only acceptable if **the numbers are real and verifiable**. Making up numbers for a copy example normalizes a bad practice and, on a real site, can be deceptive advertising. In the US, the FTC Act prohibits deceptive advertising, and the FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (in effect since October 2024) specifically bans fake reviews and testimonials. If you don't have data, use other kinds of proof: a case study with the client's permission, a named testimonial, the logo of a client who's authorized it.

### The ethical line
Persuading is helping the right person make a decision that's good for them, with true information. Manipulating is exploiting biases so someone does something that isn't good for them, or that they wouldn't do with full information. A useful test: **would you be comfortable if the reader knew exactly which technique you're using and why?** If the answer is no, it's probably manipulation.

## 5. Copy structures

Structures are starting points, not magic formulas.

### AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action
It's the oldest; it's usually attributed to adman E. St. Elmo Lewis (late 19th century), although historians debate that attribution, and the "AIDA" form spread in the early 20th century.

> **[A]** Is your online store getting traffic but hardly any sales?
> **[I]** In most cases the problem isn't traffic; it's three or four points in checkout where people drop off.
> **[D]** In a two-week audit, we find those points, fix them and measure before and after.
> **[A]** Book a 30-minute call to go over your store.

*Use it for:* landing pages, sales emails, ads with some room.

### PAS: Problem, Agitate, Solution
> **[P]** Your product is ready, but your website has been stuck for months.
> **[A]** Every week you don't launch is customers who don't know you exist and competitors taking your spot.
> **[S]** We launch your site in three weeks, with a fixed scope and a fixed price.

*Use it for:* urgent problems the reader already recognizes. *Ethical caution:* agitating isn't inventing fears or exaggerating consequences.

### FAB: Feature, Advantage, Benefit
> **[F]** Admin dashboard with role-based permissions.
> **[A]** Unlike single-user dashboards, each person sees only what they need.
> **[B]** Your team works without worrying about deleting something by accident.

*Use it for:* product pages, comparisons, technical or analytical audiences.

### BAB: Before, After, Bridge
> **[Before]** Laura tracked her store's orders in a spreadsheet and answered every shipping question by hand.
> **[After]** Today orders log themselves and customers get tracking updates automatically.
> **[Bridge]** We connected her store to her shipping system in two weeks.

*Use it for:* case studies, testimonials, aspirational copy.

### Choosing and combining
| Situation | Suggested structure |
|---|---|
| The reader has an urgent problem they recognize | PAS |
| The reader doesn't know the problem or the product | AIDA |
| The reader is comparing options with data | FAB |
| You want to show a transformation (a real case) | BAB |

A full page usually combines them: PAS in the hero, FAB in the features section, BAB in the success stories and a close with a clear call to action.

### Worked example 2 — From generic to persuasive

*Original:* "We're an agency with 10 years of experience. We offer design, development and marketing using the latest technologies. Contact us."

*Diagnosis:* it talks about the agency, not the reader; generic (anyone could say it); no proof; a vague call to action.

*Rewrite (PAS + proof + CTA):*
> **Is your website not bringing in clients?**
> If visitors show up and leave without contacting you, every month of ads is money down the drain.
> We redesign websites for professional firms so they convert: clear messaging, short forms and tracking from day one. *(Case: an accounting firm went from 4 to 11 inquiries a month in its first quarter. Fictional figure for this example; on a real site, only client numbers published with their permission.)*
> **Get a free review of your homepage** (reply within 48 hours).

(In an exercise, if you make up a figure for practice, label it as fictional. On a real site, only true data.)

## 6. Writing for people who are already half convinced

The most effective copy doesn't try to convince people who don't have the problem; it speaks to people who already feel it and are hesitating. An email to people who know your brand but haven't bought doesn't need to explain everything from scratch: it needs to resolve the doubt holding them back (price, trust, time) and make the next step easy.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Talking about the company | Talk about the outcome for the reader |
| Invented or unmeasured benefits | Only true, provable benefits |
| A generic value proposition | Who + outcome + differentiator |
| Fake scarcity or numbers | Only real limits and data |
| Forcing a structure | Choose it based on the reader and context |
| A vague CTA ("Contact us") | A specific action and what happens next |

## Summary

Copywriting aims for an action and centers the reader: it translates features into true benefits and states a specific value proposition. Cialdini's seven principles explain why certain ways of presenting information influence decisions; using them with real data is persuasion, using them with deception is manipulation. AIDA, PAS, FAB and BAB are starting structures you choose and combine based on the reader.
