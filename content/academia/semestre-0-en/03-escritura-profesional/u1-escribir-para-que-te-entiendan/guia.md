# Unit 1 — Writing to Be Understood

**Weeks 1–2 · 12 hours**

> **About the English edition.** This course was rewritten for English, not translated. The examples, the wordiness patterns and the editing checklist are the ones English readers and writers actually run into; the structure, objectives and assessment match the Spanish edition.

**Guiding question:** What does my reader need in order to understand me the first time and act?

**Estimated time:** Guide approx. 20 min (about 1,600 words at 120 wpm + 5 min of quick checks) · Exercises approx. 3 h 00 min (4 level-1 × 10 min, 2 level-2 × 25 min, 2 level-3 × 45 min) · Quiz approx. 30 min · Reflection approx. 1 h 30 min. **Active time approx. 5 h 20 min** of the 12 h planned in the syllabus. The remaining approx. 6 h 40 min is independent study: rereading, notes, reference readings and review.

> **Core idea.** Before you can persuade anyone, you have to be understood. Most professional writing fails not for lack of talent, but because it's written around the writer (what they know, what they did, what they want to say) instead of the reader (what they need, how much time they have, what they have to do next). This unit gives you a process for writing anything clearly: an email, a report, a service page.

## Objectives

- Analyze the reader, the purpose and the expected action before writing.
- Explain how people read on screens and structure scannable text.
- Apply the inverted pyramid.
- Write with clear sentences, active voice and concrete words.
- Edit your own text or AI-generated text with a checklist.

## 1. Before you write: three questions

Every piece of professional writing answers three questions, even if it never states them. Answer them in writing before you start:

1. **Who's reading?** What do they already know? What are they worried about? How much time and attention do they have? A CEO skimming twenty emails between meetings isn't the same as a developer reading documentation at their desk.
2. **What's it for?** To inform, ask, convince, apologize, instruct?
3. **What do they need to do after reading?** Reply yes or no, approve a budget, click, change a setting. If there's no action, ask yourself whether the text needs to exist.

### Worked example 1

*Situation:* a client's project will slip a week because the payment provider was slow to grant access.

- **Reader:** the client's marketing manager; she's worried about the launch she already announced to her boss; she reads on her phone.
- **Purpose:** report the delay without losing her trust.
- **Action:** confirm the new date, or decide whether to launch without online payments.

With that settled, the email almost writes itself: first the news and the decision she needs to make, then the short reason and what you're doing about it. Not the other way around.

## 2. How people read on screens

People **don't read** on screens; they **scan**. A Jakob Nielsen study (Nielsen Norman Group, 2008), based on browsing data, estimated that users read at most 28% of the words on an average web page, and that around 20% is more realistic. The same organization's eye-tracking studies described scanning patterns such as the "F-pattern": people read the first line, then a bit of the next one, and then move down the left margin looking for keywords.

Practical consequences:

- What matters goes **first**: in the text, in each paragraph, in each sentence.
- **Headings and subheadings** should inform ("Launch moves to March 20"), not decorate ("Updates").
- **Short paragraphs** (2–4 sentences), one idea per paragraph.
- **Lists** for parallel items, steps or requirements.
- **Bold** sparingly, for what a scanner must not miss.

This applies to email too: many emails are read on a phone, in a two-line preview.

## 3. The inverted pyramid

It comes from journalism: start with the conclusion or the most important point, follow with the supporting details and finish with background or secondary information.

| "School essay" structure (chronological) | Inverted pyramid |
|---|---|
| Background → development → conclusion | Conclusion → reasons/details → background |
| "On Monday we spoke with the provider, who told us… so… in conclusion, we're running late." | "We need to move the launch to March 20. Here's why…" |

If the reader stops at any point, they leave with the most important part.

## 4. Clarity at the sentence level

### 4.1 Short sentences, one idea each
There's no required length, but long sentences with several clauses force the reader to hold a lot in memory. As a practical guide, if a sentence runs past 25–30 words, see whether you can split it. (US federal plain-language guidance, at plainlanguage.gov, gives the same advice: short sentences, one main idea each.)

> ✗ "Due to the fact that the payment provider, whom we first contacted last week following Monday's meeting, has not yet granted the access required for the integration, which is essential for the launch, we find ourselves in the position of needing to reschedule the date."
> ✓ "The payment provider still hasn't given us access. We can't launch the store without it. We propose moving the launch to March 20."

### 4.2 Active voice
In the active voice, the subject does the action: *"The team reviewed the code."* In the passive, the subject receives it: *"The code was reviewed."* Active is usually shorter and makes clear who does what. Use it by default; the passive is fine when the actor doesn't matter or isn't known. Watch out for the evasive passive, too: "mistakes were made" hides who made them.

> ✗ "An error has been detected and will be corrected."
> ✓ "We found an error. We'll fix it today by 6 p.m."

### 4.3 Verbs instead of nouns
Business English loves turning verbs into nouns ("conduct a review," "carry out an implementation," "make a payment," "provide assistance"). Editors call these *nominalizations*, or "zombie nouns." Bring the verb back: *review, implement, pay, help*.

### 4.4 Concrete words
- **Specific beats generic:** "We cut load time from 6 to 2 seconds" beats "We optimized performance."
- **Cut the filler:** "it's important to note that," "in order to" (just "to"), "at this point in time" ("now"), "due to the fact that" ("because"), "in terms of."
- **Avoid jargon** your reader doesn't share. "Deploy," "SSR" or "CTR" are clear to a technical or marketing team, not to a client. Corporate buzzwords ("leverage," "synergy," "circle back") are worse: they sound like something while saying little. If you must use a technical term, explain it the first time.

### 4.5 Precision: numbers, dates, owners
"Soon," "shortly" and "ASAP" mean nothing. Write "by noon on Thursday, March 14." Say who does what: "Ana sends the design; you approve it by Friday."

## 5. Structure of common texts

**Email:** informative subject line → first sentence with the essentials or the request → necessary details → a clear next step (who, what, when). (You'll go deeper in U4.)

**Short report:** summary (conclusion and recommendation) → findings → data → appendices.

**Service page:** what it is and who it's for → what result the client gets → how it works → proof → call to action.

## 6. Editing: writing is rewriting

Keep writing and editing separate. First write a quick draft (or ask an AI for one). Then edit with this checklist:

1. Are the essentials in the first two lines?
2. Does the reader know what they need to do?
3. Does each paragraph have one idea? Do the headings inform?
4. Are there sentences over 30 words I can split?
5. Are there passives, or nouns that could be verbs?
6. Is there filler or jargon?
7. Are the numbers, dates and names correct? (Verify them, especially if an AI generated them.)
8. **Read it out loud.** Wherever you stumble, your reader will too.

### Worked example 2 — A full edit

**Original (about 80 words):**
> "Dear Valued Client, Please be advised that, with regard to the implementation of the modifications requested during last Tuesday's meeting, a thorough review of said modifications has been conducted by our development team, and it should be noted that a number of them will result in an increase in delivery timelines, and as such it would be advisable for you to indicate at your earliest convenience which of them you consider to be of the highest priority."

**Diagnosis:** filler ("please be advised," "it should be noted"), nominalizations ("implementation," "a review has been conducted"), passive voice ("has been conducted"), the important part buried at the end, no concrete date.

**Edited (about 50 words, with more useful information):**
> "Hi Laura, We reviewed the changes you asked for on Tuesday. Three of them add a week to delivery. Could you tell us by Thursday which ones are the priority? That way we keep the original date for everything else.
> The three changes are: (1) price filter, (2) Google sign-in, (3) blog."

Shorter, the request comes first, it has a date and the reader knows exactly what to decide.

## 7. Writing with AI

AI is useful for generating drafts, proposing variations and checking clarity. But it tends to produce generic text, with filler and repetitive structures ("In today's fast-paced world…," "Let's dive in"), and it can make up facts. Use it like this:

- Give it the **reader, purpose and action** (the three questions from section 1) in the prompt.
- Ask it to **edit** against specific criteria: "Rewrite with sentences under 20 words, active voice, the request in the first line."
- **Always review** with the checklist from section 6 and verify every fact.
- Adjust the tone to your voice or the brand's: readers notice when text "sounds like AI."

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Writing without knowing what the reader should do | Answer the three questions first |
| Telling it in chronological order | Inverted pyramid |
| Decorative headings | Headings that inform |
| Treating stiff formality as professionalism | Clarity and respect are what's professional |
| "Soon," "ASAP" | Specific dates and times |
| Publishing the first draft (yours or the AI's) | Edit with the checklist, and out loud |

## Summary

Clear writing starts with the reader: who they are, why you're writing and what they need to do next. On screens people scan, so the important part goes first (inverted pyramid), with informative headings, short paragraphs and lists. At the sentence level: short sentences, active voice, verbs instead of nouns, concrete words, precise numbers and dates. And writing is rewriting: separate drafting from editing, and review with a checklist, especially text generated by AI.
