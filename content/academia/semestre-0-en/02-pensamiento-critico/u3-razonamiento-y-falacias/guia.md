# Unit 3 — Logical Reasoning and Fallacies

**Weeks 5–6 · 12 hours**

**Guiding question:** Does this conclusion really follow from its reasons, or does it just sound convincing?

> **Core idea.** At work, decisions get made from arguments: in a meeting, in an email, in a proposal, in a report an AI generates. Knowing how to break an argument into its parts, judge whether the conclusion follows from the premises, and spot the most common misleading shortcuts (fallacies) lets you defend your ideas well and not get swept along by arguments that only sound good.

## Learning objectives

- Identify an argument's premises, conclusion and implicit assumptions.
- Distinguish deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning, and their degree of certainty.
- Distinguish validity (structure) from truth (content).
- Recognize and name the most common fallacies in professional settings.
- Respond to a fallacious argument constructively.

## 1. Anatomy of an argument

An **argument** is a set of statements (premises) offered as reasons to accept another statement (the conclusion).

> "Our customers are mostly small businesses (premise 1). Small businesses decide quickly and on a tight budget (premise 2). Therefore, we should offer a cheap entry plan with instant sign-up (conclusion)."

### How to break it down

1. **Find the conclusion:** what does it want you to believe or do? Clue words: *therefore, so, as a result, we should*.
2. **Find the premises:** what reasons does it give? Clues: *because, since, given that, as*.
3. **Find the implicit assumptions:** what the argument needs but doesn't say. Here, for example: "a cheap plan with instant sign-up is what most appeals to someone who decides quickly on a tight budget," and "there's no more profitable segment we should target instead."

**Implicit assumptions** are where problems usually hide. An argument with flawless explicit premises can rest on a false assumption.

### Worked example 1 — Mapping an argument from a meeting

*Argument:* "We have to be on TikTok. Every brand in our industry is already there, and our audience is young."

- **Conclusion:** we should be on TikTok.
- **Premise 1:** every brand in the industry is on TikTok.
- **Premise 2:** our audience is young.
- **Implicit assumptions:** (a) competitors being there means it works for them; (b) "young" means they use TikTok to discover brands like ours; (c) we have the capacity to produce video content consistently; (d) it's a better use of budget than the alternatives.
- **Evaluation:** P1 may be exaggerated ("every"); P2 needs data; assumptions (a) and (c) are the weakest. The conclusion may be right, but the argument doesn't support it yet.

## 2. Three types of reasoning

### Deduction: if the premises are true, the conclusion is necessarily true

> All card payments go through gateway X. Gateway X is down. → No card payments are being processed.

Deduction gives **certainty**… if the premises are true. This is where a key distinction comes in:

- **Validity:** the conclusion follows from the premises (the structure is correct).
- **Truth:** the premises match reality.

An argument can be **valid with false premises**: "All designers can code; Laura is a designer; therefore, Laura can code." The structure is flawless; the first premise is false. A **sound** argument is valid and has true premises.

### Induction: from observed cases to a probable generalization

> The last 40 emails sent on Tuesdays at 10 a.m. had a better open rate than those sent at other times. → Sending on Tuesdays at 10 a.m. probably gets better opens.

Induction gives **probability**, not certainty. Its strength depends on how many cases there are, how representative they are, and whether there are alternative explanations (Unit 2). The generalization can fail if conditions change (for example, the audience).

### Abduction: inference to the best explanation

> Sales dropped on the very day of the update, and only on Safari. → The best explanation is a bug introduced by the update that affects Safari.

This is what a good diagnosis does (and what you did in Unit 2): out of several possible explanations, pick the one that best fits the data, *provisionally*, and test it. Abduction produces hypotheses, not final conclusions.

| Type | Goes from… to… | Certainty | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deduction | general rules → case | Necessary (if premises are true) | Applying policies, program logic |
| Induction | cases → general rule | Probable | Learning from data, metrics |
| Abduction | observations → best explanation | Provisional | Diagnosis, investigation |

## 3. Fallacies: arguments that look good and aren't

A **fallacy** is a reasoning error that makes an argument seem stronger than it is. It isn't always intentional. These are the most common at work, with examples from digital settings:

### Fallacies of relevance (they attack something else)

- **Ad hominem:** attacking the person instead of the argument. *"What would she know about strategy? She's been here six months."* Tenure doesn't answer whether her proposal is good.
- **Straw man:** distorting the other person's argument to refute it more easily. *A: "We should review our ad spend." B: "So you want us to disappear from the internet."*
- **Appeal to (improper) authority:** using an authority outside their field, or as the only evidence. *"A famous founder said it on a podcast, so we have to do it."* Citing experts is reasonable; using them as a substitute for evidence isn't.
- **Appeal to popularity (ad populum):** *"Everyone uses this tool; it must be the best one for us."*
- **Appeal to tradition or to novelty:** *"We've always done it this way"* / *"It's the latest thing; we have to adopt it."*

### Fallacies of structure or evidence

- **False dilemma:** presenting only two options when there are more. *"Either we rebuild the whole website or we keep losing customers."* (What about fixing the top three problems?)
- **Slippery slope:** claiming one step will inevitably lead to a chain of extreme consequences without justifying each link. *"If we give this client a discount, everyone will ask for discounts and we'll go under."*
- **Hasty generalization:** concluding from too few cases. *"Two customers complained about the new menu; nobody likes it."*
- **Post hoc (after this, therefore because of this):** *"We changed the logo and sales went up; the logo worked."* (Unit 2: correlation is not causation.)
- **Loaded question:** a question that takes something debatable for granted. *"Why does the design team always delay projects?"*
- **Begging the question (circular reasoning):** the conclusion is in the premises. *"This vendor is the most reliable because it's the one we trust most."*
- **Sunk cost as an argument:** *"We've already put six months into this platform; we can't switch."* What's been spent won't come back; the question is what makes sense from here on. (You'll see it again as a bias in Unit 4.)
- **Equivocation:** using a word in two senses. *"The client asked for a 'simple' website, so we delivered it without search or filters"* (when "simple" meant "easy to use").

### Worked example 2 — Analyzing an email full of fallacies

> "Team: Martha's proposal to switch project management tools makes no sense. She herself admitted she never used the current one in depth. Besides, if we switch now, we'll want to switch everything every month. Every serious agency uses ours. Either we keep it or we go back to spreadsheet chaos."

| Excerpt | Fallacy | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "She herself admitted she never used the current one in depth" | Ad hominem (partly) | Her experience with the tool may be relevant, but it doesn't answer the proposal's arguments |
| "We'll want to switch everything every month" | Slippery slope | It doesn't justify why one change would lead to constant changes |
| "Every serious agency uses ours" | Ad populum + generalization | No data; besides, popular doesn't mean suitable |
| "Either we keep it or we go back to chaos" | False dilemma | It ignores the proposed option and others |

*Constructive response:* "Let's separate the proposal from who's making it: what specific problems would switching solve, and what would it cost? We can compare both tools on three criteria and try the new one on a pilot project."

## 4. How to respond to a fallacy without making enemies

Naming the fallacy ("that's an ad hominem!") rarely helps in a meeting. These work better:

1. **Restate the core point:** "If I understand correctly, your concern is the cost of switching. Is that right?"
2. **Ask for the evidence:** "Do we have data on how many agencies like ours use it, and with what results?"
3. **Open up options:** "Besides those two alternatives, what about a pilot?"
4. **Go back to the criteria:** "What criteria are we going to decide on?"

Above all, **apply these tools to your own arguments first.** The goal isn't winning arguments; it's reaching better decisions.

## 5. Reasoning and AI-generated text

Language models produce fluent, well-structured arguments, which makes them especially persuasive, even when they contain logical leaps, generalizations or made-up premises. When an AI gives you a recommendation:

- Pull out the conclusion and the premises.
- Ask what implicit assumptions it has.
- Check the factual premises (Unit 4).
- Ask for the opposing argument: "Now argue the opposite position just as strongly." If the counterargument is just as convincing, the decision depends on data you don't have yet.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Judging an argument by who makes it | Evaluate premises and structure |
| Confusing validity with truth | Check both: structure and facts |
| Treating induction as certainty | State your level of confidence |
| Calling out a fallacy in public | Restate, ask for evidence, open up options |
| Spotting fallacies only in others | Review your own arguments first |

## Summary

An argument has premises, a conclusion and implicit assumptions, and problems usually hide in the last of these. Deduction gives certainty if the premises are true (validity + truth = soundness); induction gives probability; abduction, the best provisional explanation. Fallacies make weak arguments look strong; recognizing them protects your decisions, and answering them with questions rather than labels improves conversations.
