# Unit 3 — Quiz

Key in `../claves/u3-clave-cuestionario.md`. **Part A [AUTO]** · **Part B [HUMAN]**

1. A valid argument is one in which:
   a) Everyone present agrees. b) The conclusion follows from the premises. c) There's data backing each premise. d) The premises are all true.
2. A sound argument is:
   a) Inductive and based on many cases. b) Persuasive to whoever hears it. c) Long, with many supporting quotes. d) Valid and with true premises.
3. Induction provides:
   a) Probable conclusions. b) Certain conclusions. c) Valid conclusions. d) No useful conclusion.
4. Choosing the best explanation for a set of symptoms is:
   a) Deduction b) Induction c) Abduction d) A fallacy
5. "We have to keep the vendor; we've always worked with them" is:
   a) A straw man fallacy b) An appeal to tradition c) A sound and valid argument d) A hasty generalization
6. "You want to cut the design budget, so you don't care about quality" is:
   a) Ad hominem b) Begging the question c) Post hoc d) Straw man
7. Implicit assumptions matter because:
   a) They don't matter if the conclusion is clear. b) They're always true by definition. c) They're the conclusions of the argument. d) That's where weak points usually hide.
8. Citing an expert in their own field:
   a) Is always an appeal-to-authority fallacy. b) Is reasonable support, not a substitute for evidence. c) Is off-limits in a professional debate. d) Proves the conclusion definitively.
9. The best reaction to a fallacy in a meeting is usually to:
   a) Restate their point and ask for evidence. b) Name the fallacy out loud in front of everyone. c) Point out the other person's past mistakes. d) Ignore the issue and move on.
10. Faced with a fluent recommendation from an AI, a good practice is to:
    a) Ask it to expand on it at greater length. b) Ask it to repeat it in other words. c) Ask for the opposing argument and review the premises. d) Accept it if it's well written and clear.
