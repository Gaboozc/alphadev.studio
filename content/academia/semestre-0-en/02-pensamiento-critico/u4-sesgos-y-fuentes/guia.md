# Unit 4 — Cognitive Biases and Evaluating Sources

**Weeks 7–8 · 12 hours**

**Guiding question:** How can my own mind fool me, and how do I decide whether a source (or an AI) deserves trust?

**Estimated time:** Guide approx. 20 min (about 1,750 words at 120 wpm + 5 min of quick checks) · Exercises approx. 2 h 50 min (3 level-1 × 10 min, 2 level-2 × 25 min, 2 level-3 × 45 min) · Quiz approx. 30 min · Reflection approx. 1 h 30 min. **Effective total approx. 5 h 10 min** of the 12 h planned in the syllabus. The remaining approx. 6 h 50 min is independent study: rereading, note-taking, reference reading and review.

> **Core idea.** Even when you reason well (Unit 3), your mind takes shortcuts that, in certain situations, produce systematic errors: **cognitive biases**. And even when your mind works well, the information you receive can be false, incomplete or fabricated, increasingly by AI tools. This unit gives you two defenses: knowing your biases so you can design countermeasures, and a method for evaluating sources and AI outputs before you use them.

## Learning objectives

- Explain the distinction between fast thinking (System 1) and slow thinking (System 2).
- Recognize the most common biases in digital decisions: confirmation, anchoring, availability, representativeness, survivorship, sunk cost and overconfidence.
- Apply concrete countermeasures to each bias.
- Evaluate a source with SIFT, lateral reading and CRAAP.
- Verify an AI output with a systematic protocol.

## 1. Two modes of thinking

In *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), the psychologist Daniel Kahneman popularized a useful distinction:

- **System 1:** fast, automatic, intuitive, effortless. It recognizes faces, completes "peanut butter and…," reacts to a headline.
- **System 2:** slow, deliberate, effortful. It works out 17 × 24, compares two quotes, reviews a contract.

System 1 isn't "bad": it lets us function. The problem is that it answers confidently even questions that call for System 2, and System 2, being lazy, tends to accept the answer. Biases are largely System 1 answers that nobody checked.

> Classic test: a bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost? The intuitive answer ($0.10) is wrong: it's $0.05 (bat $1.05 + ball $0.05 = $1.10).

In 1974, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published in *Science* ("Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases") the work that described three **heuristics** (judgment shortcuts): representativeness, availability and anchoring. Since then, research has cataloged many biases; here we'll cover the most useful ones for work.

## 2. Common biases and their countermeasures

### Confirmation bias
Seeking, interpreting and remembering the information that confirms what we already believe.
*Example:* you're convinced the new design is better, and you only look at the metrics that went up.
*Countermeasure:* before looking at data, write down what result would change your mind. Actively look for the evidence against (as in Unit 2: several hypotheses).

### Anchoring
A first number, even an arbitrary one, shapes later estimates.
*Example:* the client says "we were thinking around $500," and your quote ends up near $500, even though the work is worth three times that. Conversely, whoever puts a high figure on the table first in a proposal influences the negotiation (you'll use this, ethically, in *Professional Writing*, Unit 4).
*Countermeasure:* estimate on your own before hearing any figures; build the price from costs and value, not from the first number you heard.

### Availability
Judging how frequent or likely something is by how easy it is to recall examples.
*Example:* after one loud complaint on social media, the team believes "all our customers are unhappy."
*Countermeasure:* ask for the base rate: how many complaints out of how many customers? Use data, not memories.

### Representativeness
Judging by resemblance to a stereotype while ignoring base rates.
*Example:* "This candidate talks like a startup founder; he's bound to be a great product leader."
*Countermeasure:* ask "how often does this happen in general?" and evaluate against criteria defined in advance.

### Survivorship bias
Drawing conclusions only from the cases that "survived" a filter, ignoring those that didn't.
*Classic example:* during World War II, the statistician Abraham Wald reasoned that armor should go on the areas of returning planes that showed *no* hits, because planes hit there hadn't made it back.
*Digital example:* "Every successful startup founder I know dropped out of college" — you don't see the thousands who also dropped out and failed. "This AI tool's success stories" are, by definition, a filtered sample.
*Countermeasure:* ask "where are the ones who failed?" and "who picked these examples?"

### Sunk cost fallacy
Continuing to invest in something because of what's already been invested, not what you expect to get.
*Example:* keeping a campaign that isn't working "because we already paid for the video production."
*Countermeasure:* ask "if I were starting from scratch today, would I invest in this?" Before starting, define kill criteria ("if by week 4 the cost per customer exceeds X, we stop").

### Overconfidence and the planning fallacy
We overestimate what we know and underestimate how long a project will take, even knowing that previous ones ran late.
*Countermeasure:* take the "outside view": how long did similar projects take? Express estimates as ranges ("between 3 and 5 weeks") and revisit them with data.

### Worked example 1 — Diagnosing biases in a decision

> "Our competitor launched an app and it's doing great; I saw it in a post by their CEO. Our biggest client mentioned it too. We already have half an app built from the project we canceled last year, so let's finish it. I estimate it'll be ready in a month."

| Excerpt | Bias | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| "It's doing great; I saw it in a post by their CEO" | Survivorship / interested source | Look for independent data (downloads, reviews) |
| "Our biggest client mentioned it" | Availability | How many clients are asking for it? A short survey |
| "We already have half an app built" | Sunk cost | Would we build this from scratch today? |
| "It'll be ready in a month" | Planning fallacy | How long did the first half take? Use a range |

**Warning:** knowing about biases doesn't make you immune. Research shows we spot biases more easily in others than in ourselves. That's why useful countermeasures are **processes** (checklists, criteria defined in advance, review by someone else), not willpower.

## 3. Evaluating sources

### SIFT (Mike Caulfield, 2019)
A fast method for the internet, designed for deciding in minutes:

1. **S — Stop.** Before you share or believe, stop. Do you know the source? What does it make you feel?
2. **I — Investigate the source.** Who's behind it? What are their interests?
3. **F — Find better coverage.** Do other reliable sources say the same thing?
4. **T — Trace claims.** Follow the quote, data point or image back to its original source. Does it say what the article claims?

### Lateral reading
Instead of reading a page top to bottom to judge whether it's reliable (vertical reading), open other tabs and search for what others say about that source. Professional fact-checkers do this: they leave the site right away. A website can look very professional and be propaganda or a sales page.

### CRAAP (Sarah Blakeslee, 2004)
A more detailed checklist, useful for academic work or reports:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| **Currency** | When was it published? Is it still current? (In tech, two years can be a long time.) |
| **Relevance** | Does it answer your question? Who is it written for? |
| **Authority** | Who's the author? What are their credentials? |
| **Accuracy** | Is it backed by evidence? Can it be verified? |
| **Purpose** | To inform, sell, persuade? Is there a conflict of interest? |

### Worked example 2 — Evaluating a statistic

*Statistic found:* "73% of consumers abandon a site that takes more than 2 seconds to load," on a hosting company's blog.

1. **Stop:** the statistic suits whoever published it (they sell speed).
2. **Investigate the source:** corporate blog, commercial purpose.
3. **Better coverage:** you search for the statistic; it's repeated on dozens of blogs, almost always without a link to a study.
4. **Trace:** if you can't find the original study with its method and date, **don't use the figure**. If you find it, check the date, the sample, and whether it measures what's being claimed.

*Conclusion:* "Speed affects abandonment" is backed by serious sources; the specific figure is unknown. Better to cite a verifiable study or measure on your own site.

This pattern (a striking statistic repeated without a source) is very common in digital marketing. In the *Professional Writing* course you'll see why you must never make up figures in your copy.

## 4. Evaluating AI outputs

Language models generate plausible text, not necessarily true text (*Working with AI*, Unit 1). Typical errors: made-up data and citations ("hallucinations"), outdated information, wrong calculations, overconfidence and reasoning with leaps. They also tend to agree with you if your question already hints at an answer.

### Verification protocol (apply it according to the risk)

1. **Classify the risk:** what happens if this is wrong? A brainstorming draft: low risk. A figure in a client proposal, legal advice or a medical fact: high risk.
2. **Pull out the verifiable claims:** dates, figures, names, quotes, links, tool versions.
3. **Verify against primary sources:** official documentation, the original study, the published law. A link generated by the AI may not exist or may not say what it claims: open it.
4. **Recalculate** the numbers with a calculator or spreadsheet.
5. **Ask for the counterargument** and the limitations ("What could be wrong with this answer?").
6. **Ask neutral questions:** "Is X or Y better?" instead of "X is better, right?"
7. **Document** what you verified and how, especially if others will use the result.

### Worked example 3

You ask an AI for "e-commerce usage statistics in the US for a proposal." It gives you five figures with study names. Applying the protocol: high risk (it's going to the client); you pull out the five figures; you look up each study: two exist and match, one exists but the figure is from a different year, two don't turn up. Result: you use two figures with their source and date, correct one and delete two. Without verification, the proposal would have carried false data with your name on it.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Believing that knowing your biases is enough | Design processes: criteria set in advance, peer review |
| Judging a website by how it looks | Lateral reading |
| Citing a figure repeated on many blogs | Trace it back to the original study |
| Trusting links an AI gives you | Open them and check they say what's claimed |
| Verifying everything with the same effort | Match the verification to the risk |

## Summary

System 1 lets us function but produces predictable biases: confirmation, anchoring, availability, representativeness, survivorship, sunk cost and overconfidence. Effective countermeasures are processes, not willpower. For outside information, SIFT and lateral reading let you decide quickly, and CRAAP allows a more detailed analysis. AI outputs are verified according to risk, by pulling out the checkable claims and testing them against primary sources.
