# 07 · Calibrated Peer Review

**Id:** `s0-c2-u2-pares` · **Format:** peer review with prior calibration · **Time:** approx. 60 min (15 for calibration + 2 reviews × 20 min + 5 for feedback on feedback) [INFERENCE] · **Assessment:** [HUMAN]. What's assessed is the **quality of your feedback**, not the score you give.

**What gets reviewed:** **exercise 3.1** from the original unit (`../u2-causas-hipotesis-evidencia/ejercicios.md`): the 200–250-word analysis of "since we started posting on LinkedIn, requests for proposals have risen 40%." You've done it, and so has everyone in your group.

**Why:**
- Peer assessment improves performance compared with no assessment (g = 0.31) [VERIFIED: Double et al. 2020, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09510-3].
- Coursera uses rubric-based peer review [VERIFIED: `benchmark.md`, section 6].
- The most useful feedback is about the **process** and **self-regulation**, not about the person [VERIFIED: Hattie and Timperley 2007, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/003465430298487].
- Reviewing other people's work against criteria also teaches you to review your own [INFERENCE].

## Step 1 · Calibration (15 min)

Before reviewing your classmates, score these two sample answers with the rubric in step 2. Then compare with the instructor's scores at the end of this file. If you're off by more than 2 points in total, reread the descriptors before continuing.

> Both answers are **fictional**, written for calibration.

**Answer A**
> "40% is a good result and shows that LinkedIn works, but it could be due to other things. For example, it could be chance, or there could be more demand in those months. Also, we don't know how many requests there were before. We should keep posting and see if the number keeps going up. If it keeps going up, then we could say it works. We could also ask clients how they found us."

**Answer B**
> "The claim is: posting on LinkedIn (A) increased requests for proposals (B) by 40%. I see three alternatives. (1) Seasonal confounding: if the comparison is January vs. March, the start of the fiscal year also increases requests; we'd need to compare with March of last year. (2) Other simultaneous changes: was there a campaign, a client who referred us or a new website? If requests rose in channels unrelated to LinkedIn, the effect isn't LinkedIn's. (3) Small base: if we went from 10 to 14 requests, the 40% could be chance. To test it: (a) add 'How did you hear about us?' to the form, with options in random order; (b) use UTM links on every post; (c) if we want something stronger, alternate months with and without posting for six months and compare. Today the evidence is level 3: data without comparison."

## Step 2 · Rubric for exercise 3.1

| Criterion | 3 · Proficient | 2 · Partial | 1 · Insufficient |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alternatives** | ≥ 3 **distinct**, concrete alternatives for *this* case | 2 alternatives, or 3 generic ones ("it could be chance") | 0–1 |
| **Distinguishing data** | For each alternative, says what data would confirm or rule it out | For some | None |
| **Testing** | Proposes a feasible way to measure the real effect, stronger than "keep watching" | Proposes something, but weak or vague | Nothing, or "keep posting and see" |
| **Level of evidence** | Rates the current evidence and doesn't conclude more than it allows | Hints at it | Concludes "it works" or "it doesn't" without nuance |

Minimum 4 points, maximum 12.

## Step 3 · Review 2 classmates (2 × 20 min)

The review is anonymous: you don't know whose work it is, and they won't know it's you [INFERENCE: a design decision; it reduces bias toward friends]. For each piece of work:
1. **Score** each criterion and quote the sentence from the work that justifies your score.
2. **Write 3 comments** using these templates:
   - **Task** (what's good or missing): "On [criterion], your answer ___ because ___."
   - **Process** (how to improve): "To move up a level on [criterion], try ___ (for example, ___)."
   - **Self-regulation** (how to check it yourself): "Before submitting, you could ask yourself ___."
3. **Not allowed:**
   - comments about the person ("you're really good," "you obviously didn't study");
   - empty comments ("good," "improve writing");
   - rewriting the work for the author.

**Example of a well-written process comment:**
> "On *distinguishing data*: you say it could be the season, but you don't say how to find out. Try stating which period you'd compare against: the same month last year, for example."

## Step 4 · Feedback on feedback (5 min)

When you get your two reviews:
1. Rate how useful each one was: **useful / somewhat useful / not very useful**, with one sentence saying why.
2. Write down **one** thing you'll change in your answer, and do it. The revised version is submitted with the unit reflection.

## How this activity is assessed

| Criterion (of your feedback) | 3 | 2 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | Your score is within ≤ 1 point of the instructor's on the reviewed work (sample) | 2–3 points | > 3 points |
| **Justification** | Every score quotes the text | Some | None |
| **Process feedback** | Concrete and actionable, with an example | Actionable, but no example | Vague |
| **Respect** | No personal judgments | — | Contains personal judgments |

- The instructor reviews a sample of the reviews, plus every one the recipient marks as "not very useful" [INFERENCE: consistent with `plan-revision.md`, section 5].
- The activity counts toward the unit's exercise grade. Gabe decides its exact weight.
