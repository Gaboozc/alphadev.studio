## Where AI projects die

Not in the tech. They die in the promise: a client expecting something that was never possible and a budget that didn't account for the part that turned out to be 70% of the work.

### The three questions before quoting

**Does the data exist?** Specifically: does it exist today, is it accessible, is its quality high enough, and does the client have the right to use it? If the answer is "we'll generate it as we go," the project isn't what it looks like: it's a data project first.

**What's the success criterion and who judges it?** "That it works well" isn't a criterion. "That it answers at least 85% of the 50 agreed-upon questions correctly" is, because it can be verified and debated before starting.

**What happens when it's wrong?** Every AI system gets some percentage of cases wrong. If the client doesn't have an answer for that, they haven't understood what they're buying, and that conversation is better held up front.

### What has to be said out loud

A model-powered system **isn't always right**. It can be bounded, measured and improved, but not guaranteed at 100%. A client expecting perfection will be unhappy with an excellent system.

The honest way to frame it: *"The system will resolve most cases and escalate the rest to a person with context already gathered. We'll measure that percentage and improve it."* That you can deliver; "it'll answer everything right" you can't.

### The scoped proof of concept

Before the full project, two weeks with minimal scope and a written success criterion. It does three things: verifies the data exists and works, measures for real, and gives the client something to touch before committing the big budget.

If the proof doesn't meet the criterion, that's the result: stop or rethink. Having that exit is part of what makes it valuable.

### What to put in the proposal

- The problem in one sentence, with the number it needs to improve.
- What the system does and, explicitly, **what it doesn't do**.
- The measurable success criterion and who verifies it.
- Where the data comes from and who supplies it.
- What happens to cases the system doesn't resolve.
- Estimated operating cost and who pays it.
- What happens if the model provider raises prices or retires the model.

The last two almost never appear in proposals and are the ones that cause conflicts.

### Warning signs

- "We want something with AI" with no problem behind it.
- A third party holds the data and hasn't said yes yet.
- The success criterion is the opinion of someone who isn't in the meetings.
- They expect to replace an entire team on day one.
- The budget includes nothing for ongoing operations afterward.

## Tasks
- Write a proposal for a real project including the section on what it does NOT do
- Define a measurable success criterion with its number and verification method
- Calculate monthly operating cost and decide how it's billed
- List the project's top three risks and what you'd do if they materialize

## Tip

The most valuable section of an AI proposal is the one on what the system won't do. Writing it protects the client from expecting too much and protects you from being held to something never agreed on.
